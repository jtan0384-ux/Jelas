What Jelas does

Explains

A plain-language reason for the restriction, delivered the moment it happens, built from the bank's own fraud and verification output.

Resolves

A self-serve route back in, covering the three failures that generate the most support contacts: new devices, changed numbers, and codes that never arrive.

Records

An auditable trail of what was restricted, why, what the customer was told, and how long resolution took.

Jelas never makes or overrides the risk decision. It handles what happens next.

The controls are working. That is the problem.

Malaysian banks lost RM2.8 billion to scams in 2025 and intervened to prevent a further RM1.2 billion. Automated fraud, anti-money-laundering and identity controls are the reason. Those controls are correct far more often than not — and when they misfire, the customer is locked out of their own money with no reason given and nobody to ask.

The national picture

Bank Negara Malaysia's 2025 annual report records RM2.8 billion in scam losses and RM1.2 billion averted through intervention. Every one of those interventions is a control acting on a live account.

The complaints that escalate

The Financial Markets Ombudsman Service handled 11,431 new complaints in 2025, with unauthorised banking and e-money transactions among the most common dispute types. Because the Ombudsman is a last resort, that figure is a floor — most cases never reach it.

Nobody publishes the two bands above it. That gap is why we ran a survey.

Jelas reads the bank's systems. It never writes to them. The restriction decision stays with the bank; Jelas manages the consequence.

Explained restrictions

The customer sees a plain-language reason and an expected resolution window at the moment of restriction. No jargon reaches them: an AML velocity flag becomes "we need to confirm a recent transfer".

Self-serve recovery

Guided routes for a new device, a changed phone number, and a verification code that never arrived. Identity checks step up in proportion to the risk of the case.

False-positive triage

Each restriction is scored on how likely it is to be a legitimate customer caught by a correct rule. Low-risk cases route to expedited review, shortening hold times without touching the AML decision.

Conduct audit trail

Every case records what was restricted, why, what the customer was told, and how long resolution took — a defensible file for complaint handling and conduct reporting.

Prototype and production

This site is a working click-through of the production interface, built as static HTML and CSS with a small JavaScript router. A production build would use a React frontend, a Python service layer reading bank fraud and KYC systems over REST APIs, and an append-only case store. No customer data is processed by this prototype and no live bank system is connected to it.

You already pay for every lockout. You pay twice.

A restricted customer contacts support an average of 2.5 times before the case closes. At a fully loaded cost of RM7.79 per contact, a bank with 480,000 customers spends around RM140,000 a year handling access failures — before counting the customers who leave.

Why we price this way

Enterprise regulatory-technology pricing is not published, so there is no benchmark to copy. We priced against what the bank avoids instead: roughly a third of the operational cost Jelas removes. That leaves the client a return of about three times, and it scales with institution size rather than seat count.

How we compete

Detection vendors are paid to flag risk

CTOS Digital, Experian, GBG, Innov8tif and Tookitaki all compete on finding problems. None of them sells the resolution of the access denial their systems produce.

We integrate, we do not replace

Jelas reads the fraud, AML and eKYC systems a bank already runs. No control is removed, no decision overridden, no compliance re-approval triggered.

Eight weeks, not eighteen months

A paid pilot with agreed success metrics before any annual contract. Enterprise procurement is the barrier in this market; a small scoped pilot is the way through it.

Built at Monash University Malaysia.

Jelas began as an individual project in BFM 3130, built by coding 71 public reviews of Malaysian fintech applications and finding that 26 of them described the same failure: a customer denied access to their own money, with no explanation and no way to resolve it. The group selected the concept, surveyed [N] Malaysians to test how common that experience is, and built the prototype you have just used.

Step 1 — The moment it happens

Note what this screen does not say. There is no apology, no error code, and no mention of fraud. It leads with the balance being safe, because that is the customer's first fear.

In the coded reviews behind this project, the single most common complaint was not the restriction itself. It was being told nothing about it.

Why your transfers are paused

A transfer of RM2,000 on 28 September went to an account you have not sent money to before, from a device we did not recognise. Our systems paused outgoing transfers while we confirm it was you.

This is a standard check. It does not mean anything is wrong with your account.

Most checks like this are resolved within 2 working days. You can resolve it now instead.

Step 2 — A reason, in plain language

This screen is the product. "Unrecognised device, first-time payee, velocity rule triggered" became one sentence a person can act on, and the bank's own systems supplied every fact in it.

The line stating that nothing is wrong with the account is deliberate. A restriction notice that reads like an accusation produces a complaint; one that reads like a check produces a resolution.

Step 4 — Proportionate checks

The verification asked for matches the risk of the case. A routine device change needs less than a first-time payee for a large sum, and the triage score decides which.

The line about not storing biometrics is there on purpose. A product handling identity data should say what it does not keep, and it answers the obvious question about data protection before anyone asks it.
